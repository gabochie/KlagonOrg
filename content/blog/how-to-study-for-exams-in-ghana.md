# How to Study for Exams in Ghana: A System That Actually Works

I've watched my fair share of exam stories in Ghana — the all-nighters, the "I'll start tomorrow" that never starts, and the panic re-reading of textbooks on the morning of the paper.

The students who perform best are rarely the most intelligent. They're the ones with a **repeatable system**. This guide gives you one — a 6-week exam cycle built around the same methods that work for BECE, WASSCE, and university finals.

## The exam is a game. Study it like one.

Every Ghanaian exam follows patterns: past questions repeat, marking schemes are predictable, and examiners reward structured answers over long ones.

The student who has practised ten past questions understands the exam *as an exam* — the wording, the timing, the marks. The student who only re-reads the textbook understands the *content* but not the *game*. That's why I've seen classmates who "know their notes" still choke, while classmates who drilled past questions walked in calm.

**Rule one: past questions are your syllabus.** If you only have limited time, spend it on past questions, not on re-reading chapters.

## The 6-week exam cycle

Six weeks out, work backwards. Here's the system in four phases:

| Phase | When | What you do |
|---|---|---|
| **Sprint** | Week 6–5 | Do one past question per subject per week. Mark it hard and honest. |
| **Fix** | Week 4–3 | For every question you lost marks on, write the correct method in your own words. Re-study only those topics. |
| **Drill** | Week 2 | Repeat the same past paper under exam timing. No open book. |
| **Polish** | Week 1 | Memorise the small stuff: formulas, dates, definitions, key words. Practise 3 past questions per subject. |

That's it. No 14-hour days, no "serious Dou" that collapses after a week. Six deliberate weeks beats three months of unfocused slog.

## Active recall beats re-reading — always

Re-reading feels productive because it's easy. But it's the weak sister of studying.

**Active recall** is the technique of testing yourself instead of staring at notes:

- Read one page. Close the book.
- Write or say everything you remember.
- Check. Re-learn what you missed.
- Repeat.

A 25-minute recall session produces more retained knowledge than two hours of reading. The discomfort of "I can't remember it" is the exact feeling of learning happening.

## A day-by-day daily routine (even while school continues)

You don't need to quit everything to study. You need a daily slot:

| Time | Block |
|---|---|
| 5:30 – 6:00 | One flash-card round of the previous day's hardest topic |
| After school | One past-question practice (20–30 min) |
| Evening | Mark it, fix it, note the gaps (15 min) |
| Night | 10-minute recall of today's gains, in your own words |

Use a phone timer in 25/5 cycles: 25 minutes of focus, 5 minutes off. Phone is for the timer, not the feed — put the SIM card to work loading past questions, then switch on data block or airplane mode during study blocks.

## The night before and the morning of

The night before: **stop studying by 8pm.** Review only your one-page summary sheets — the condensed notes, formulas, and key words you built during the week. Sleep is where memory is wired in. Staying up "to be sure" actively *lowers* recall.

The morning of: eat something, arrive early, and in the hall, read the whole paper first. Answer the questions with the most marks first, then the ones you know confidently, then the hard ones. A structured script like this calms the exam hall panic that steals marks from over-prepared students.

## Where past questions live (free, no cost)

- Your school's past-question booklets and library copies.
- The WAEC official past questions portal.
- WhatsApp study groups — but beware: joining a group that shares *content* is useful; joining one that shares *doubt* is noise. Leave the noise.
- The KlagonOrg Learning Hub's financial literacy and career courses teach the same score-keeping mentality — track your marks the way a learner tracks progress: visibly, weekly, honestly.

The exam isn't only a test of the subject, it's a test of your *system*. Build the system, run it for six weeks, and walk into the hall knowing you've already answered these questions before. That's not luck. That's preparation.
