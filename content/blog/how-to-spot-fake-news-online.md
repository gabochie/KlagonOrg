# How to Spot Fake News and Misinformation Online

"That teacher in Accra beat a student to death" — shared 40,000 times on WhatsApp in an hour. Next day, the school confirmed the teacher never existed, and the story was stolen from a different country, three years old.

Fake news isn't a distant American problem. It's in our group chats, buying land and stealing trust. The good news: misinformation runs on predictable habits, and you can build a single reflex that catches almost all of it — **the STOP test**.

## Why fake news travels so fast in Ghana

Fake stories move faster than true ones for three reasons:

1. **Outrage and fear sell.** "You MUST see this" travels because emotion travels faster than accuracy.
2. **We forward before we verify.** The WhatsApp culture rewards being first — "I saw it first" — not being right.
3. **Real-looking uniforms.** Fake posts now use government logos, "News One" watermarks, and official-sounding language. If it *looks* official, we assume it *is* official.

None of this is your fault, but all of it is your responsibility the second you press **forward**.

## The STOP test: verify any story in 60 seconds

Before sharing anything that makes you angry, afraid, or proud, run **S-T-O-P**:

- **S — Source.** Who posted it first? Is it a known, established outlet — or a random "ViralGhana247" page created three days ago? Anonymous pages have no reputation to protect. Scroll the page; if every post screams "SHARE", it's an alarm, not a newspaper.
- **T — Time.** Is this actually new? Old news presented as today's is one of the most common tricks. Search the headline; if the story is months old, it isn't news.
- **O — Origin.** Where did the "photo" or video come from? Reverse-image-search the photo (Google Lens) or check the video's date. A 2018 video from Nigeria wearing a "2026 story from Accra" caption is not evidence.
- **P — Purpose.** Why is this being sent to you? To inform, or to make you act fast, pay, share, or panic? Content designed to trigger action without verification has one purpose — to use you.

Sixty seconds of STOP catches nine out of ten fakes.

## Quick red flags to memorise

Train your eye with this checklist:

- **No named journalist or outlet.** Real journalism has bylines and websites; forwarded screenshots have neither.
- **"URGENT / SHARE WITH EVERYONE / FORWARD TO ALL"** — urgency and begging-for-forwards are the two loudest alarm bells.
- **Grammar and spelling chaos** in the headline — genuine outlets publish clean headlines before writing even one body sentence.
- **One photo, no other angle, no witness**, and the photo is very low quality. Real events have many photos; invented ones have one.
- **The "reported by" is a label**, not a person — "Checks by Ghana Police" with no officer or reference is decoration.
- **It tells you exactly what to feel**, rather than telling you what happened. Fans don't report; journalists report.

## Check before you forward — every time

Ghana is a WhatsApp-forwarding market, and that's where you become the influencer that matters. Make forwarding a two-step action:

1. **Run STOP in your head** (takes a minute).
2. **If it fails any letter, don't forward.** If you must forward for someone to check, say so: *"Not sure if true — please verify before sharing."*

That one label converts you from an amplifier of misinformation to a filter. Families and groups around you will start trusting your word *more* because your for sharing is your reputation.

## If you already shared it

Mistakes happen — everyone has forwarded something dodgy. Do this:

1. **Send a correction in the same group.** "The story I sent earlier is unverified/confirmed fake — here's the real report." A public correction is a public act of honesty, and it models verification for others.
2. **Use a fact-check.** Ghana has fact-checking organisations and journalists who debunk viral claims; search the claim plus the word "fact check" and you'll usually find who investigated it.
3. **Never quietly delete.** Erasing a fake without a correction lets it resurface later with fresh eyes. Corrections outlive embarrassment.

## The deeper skill: reading with a raised eyebrow

Critical thinking about news is the same skill as critical thinking about a job offer, a loan, or a business partner: **don't outsource judgement to the message itself.** Ask who benefits, what evidence exists, and what's missing. That habit — not any single app — is what separates people who use the internet from people who are used by it.
