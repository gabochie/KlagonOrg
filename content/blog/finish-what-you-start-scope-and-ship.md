# Why You Don't Finish Things — and How to Start Finishing

"I'll do it properly." — the shop that never opens.

"If I just get one more thing first..." — the course that's still "almost ready".

"I'm 80% there." — said by someone who still hasn't shipped.

We blame laziness for unfinished projects. But laziness isn't the villain — the villain is **scope**. Look at anything you abandoned and you'll see it: the plan quietly grew until finishing felt impossible, so your brain stopped the pain by calling it "not ready yet". 

Fixing this matters because in Ghana's market — digital or physical — the people who *ship* build the reputation. A finished small thing is street-credible; an abandoned perfect thing is invisible. Here's the system for actually finishing, from someone who coordinates community projects where "finishing" means real trees in the ground and real kids in a coding club.

## Admit the real diagnosis: scope

Untouched reasons projects die:

- **The plan grew.** "A simple banner" became "branding for the whole business".
- **Perfection hid inside the plan.** You add features not to serve users but to delay the moment of judgement.
- **The finish line moved.** Every week there's "one more important thing" before you can call it done.
- **No one was watching.** Projects with no deadline and no witness die silently — there's no cost to not finishing.

If your project had at least two of these, it wasn't a willpower problem. It was a *sizing* problem. And sizing has a cure.

## The shrink-to-ship rule

Build the smallest version that could be called "done". Then build exactly that.

A "done" thing is:

- One product, not the full line.
- One page, not the full site.
- One workshop, not the whole syllabus.
- One week's worth, not a lifetime's.

Ask yourself: **what's the smallest thing I could deliver this month that a customer, client, club, or examiner would accept?** That's your project. Everything else lives on a "version 2" list you're not allowed to build yet.

This is not lowering standards — it's raising the odds. A small finished thing produces the money, the feedback, and the confidence that bigger versions need. Big unfinished things produce only guilt.

## The 90% rule

Here's the pattern every unfinished project follows: the first 70% is thrilling, the 70–90% zone gets boring, and the final 10% — the polish — is where attention dies.

So give yourself one hard rule: **"90% good" counts as done for this round.** For a poster, a course, a product, a report — ship at 90%: it works, it's decent, it's *real*. Note the feedback you'd fix in v2, and ship. The final 10% of polish has a savage cost (weeks of fiddling) and a tiny return (nobody inspects it as closely as your imagination does).

Clarity: this rule is for products, posts, projects, and processes — not for safety, money, or people. Funerals of compromise don't apply here. But for anything else you're procrastinating on, 90% is the finish line.

## Make a date with the outside world

Private projects have no watchdog. Public ones do. Use it:

- **Set a public deadline.** Tell your WhatsApp group, your church, your mentor: "My shop opens Saturday." A public date transforms finishing from a private preference into a public promise.
- **Enrol a deadline-keeper.** One person — a friend, a member of the KlagonOrg community, an accountability partner — agrees to call you on the date and ask. Not to help, to ask. Having to *say* "I didn't" in front of a witness is a force multipliers stronger than any memo to yourself.
- **Book the room.** If it's an event or launch, rent/buy the slot. Money already spent is a magnificent motivator.

None of this is manipulation — it's engineering. You are designing the environment that makes finishing the path of least resistance, instead of the path of most pain.

## Ship, then look back — the 48-hour review

The day you ship, do nothing but sleep. The mistake people make is reviewing a fresh shipped thing with critical eyes and hating it.

Two days later, do the review that counts: three questions only.

1. What did shipping actually cost?
2. What did the world say (sales, feedback, attendance)?
3. What would v2 change?

That 48-hour review is also a lesson in *writing down* wins. Note the feeling of "I finished something" — save it. It is the rarest, most valuable fuel in a young person's career, and you'll need to read it back the next time the scope-monster whispers "just one more thing".

## Start by finishing something small this week

Pick literally the smallest thing you've been avoiding — a two-page report, a poster, a WhatsApp catalog, a project for the KlagonOrg volunteer programme you said you'd do. Shrink it to 90%, set the public date, ship it, and review it on day three.
