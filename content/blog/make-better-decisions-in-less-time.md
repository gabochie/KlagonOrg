# How to Make Better Decisions When You Have Little Time

Should I take the Accra job with the unpredictable pay, or stay home and grind my freelance side? Should I spend the GHS 200 on a course or on stock for my mini-shop? Should I reply to this WhatsApp deal now, or wait?

Young people in Ghana make dozens of these calls while standing in queues, while in class, while tired at midnight. And tired, hurried decisions are bad decisions — not because we're foolish, but because we're making them with a broken process.

Better decisions don't come from more willpower. They come from a **fast filter system**. Here are three you can start using tonight.

## Filter 1: The 10-10-10 rule (for life choices)

When a decision feels huge, ask what it looks like in **10 minutes, 10 months, and 10 years**:

- **10 minutes:** What's the immediate discomfort? (The nervy feeling of replying, the embarrassment of asking.)
- **10 months:** Will I still care about this? (Course cost, a bad job stint — absorbed by life.)
- **10 years:** Does this move my trajectory? (Skill, savings discipline, family, health — these compound.)

The trap of overthinking is that your brain anchors on the **10-minute** pain and treats it as a 10-year consequence. The 10-10-10 rule pulls your zoom lens out. Decisions that look terrifying at 10 minutes and trivial at 10 years are usually "just do it" decisions; decisions that look same-y at 10 minutes but huge at 10 years are the ones to invest in.

## Filter 2: The 3-wickets filter (for practical calls)

Too many options paralyse. Give yourself three wickets to pass before you choose:

1. **Would I do this if no one were watching?** (Removes show-off and pride-driven choices.)
2. **Does it trade short comfort for long gain?** (Removes the pure dopamine option.)
3. **Could I reverse it within a month if it fails?** (Removes the "all-in forever" gamble — most things in Ghana at your stage ARE reversible.)

Anything passing all three wickets is a fine decision *right now*. You don't need to find the perfect option — you need to eliminate the bad ones, then march. Perfection is the enemy of a decision made on time.

## Filter 3: The decision table (for money and commitments)

When it's a real trade-off — rent vs. course, this supplier vs. that, accept vs. decline — write a tiny table. Two columns, four rows, sixty seconds:

| Row | Question |
|---|---|
| Cost | What does this really cost me (money, time, energy)? |
| Upside | Best realistic outcome? |
| Downside | Worst realistic outcome — and can I survive it? |
| Deadline | By when must I decide, and what happens if I delay? |

Writing beats thinking because thinking loops; writing exposes. Most bad choices are simply decisions made without knowing the worst case. When the worst case is survivable, you're free to take the shot.

## Decide with a deadline — the 72-hour rule

Indecision *is* a decision — it's a decision to let the world choose for you. If you say "I'll think about it" to a course, a job, or a supplier, the scale tips without you.

So set a deadline: **within 72 hours, decide.** For genuinely new information, extend once — then decide. The 72-hour rule has a beautiful side effect: you will stop re-deciding things you've already decided. Endless re-deciding is where the real time, and the real confidence, leaks away.

## Decide, then improve — action beats agonising

Here's the secret of people who seem to "get lucky": their decisions aren't smarter at the moment — they *bed down* better because they take feedback and adjust. The Ghanaian trader who buys a different stock next week is a better investor than the one who waited months "for the right price" and never entered.

So run your filters, set your deadline, move — then review one month later. What did the decision actually cost, what did it return, and what would you change? That review is what upgrades you from someone who *hopes* choices are right to someone who *knows* they're right — because you measure them.
