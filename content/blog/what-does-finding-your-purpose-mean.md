# What Does It Mean to 'Find Your Purpose'? A Realistic Guide

Everywhere in youth spaces you'll hear: *"You need to find your purpose."*

The pressure is real, the language is heavy, and for many people it becomes a source of anxiety rather than direction. So let's be honest about what purpose is — and what it isn't — with a calmer, more practical approach.

## The unhelpful versions of purpose

Two readings of "purpose" cause most of the pain:

1. **The destiny version:** "There's ONE perfect thing written for you, and if you miss it, your life is wasted." This paralyses more than it guides; it persuades people to wait for certainty that never comes.
2. **The job title version:** "Your purpose is your career." But plenty of people love their church, their volunteering, or their *kind of person they are,* without any job title attached.

Both create a false test you keep failing. Neither matches life as it actually works.

## A more useful definition

Purpose doesn't have to be a thunderbolt. A workable definition:

> **Purpose is the direction you feel pulled toward, expressed in the actions you take — found by reflecting on what you cared about and doing more of it.**

Notice: it's *active,* not *discovered* (as if you'd stumble on it). It's *directional* rather than *destiny.* And it's built through practice, not through anxious searching.

## Three practical pointers to actually use

### 1. Look at your emotions, not the stars
What makes you angry? What energises you for hours without needing a reward? What would you keep doing even if it paid badly? These "tells" are the closest thing to a compass you'll find. They point you in a direction to *test,* not a destination to perfect.

### 2. Experiment, don't excavate
Don't sit alone waiting for clarity. **Do things** — join a project, try a skill, volunteer once. Each experiment teaches what fits and what doesn't. Purpose emerges from doing, far more than from thinking about doing.

### 3. Choose from your values
Pick 3 values you refuse to trade (honesty, family, fairness, growth…), and let them be the filter. Actions aligned with your values feel meaningful — that feeling, sustained over time, is what people actually call "purpose." You don't need a grand plan; you need the consistent application of what matters to you.

## The myth that should go

*"I must find my purpose before I can start anything."*

It's backwards, and it's a well-disguised form of procrastination. **You don't find purpose and then act; you act and then discover what moved you.** Small investments in many directions — a course, a project, a talk — actively *generate* clarity. Waiting does the opposite.

## Purpose in practice (a 15-minute session)

Try this once:

1. Write 3 things that recently gave you energy (people, problem, craft).
2. Write 3 things happening in your community you wish were different.
3. Find the overlap — the intersection of *what energises you* and *what the community needs* — and write it as one sentence.

One sentence. That's a working purpose you can genuinely act on this year.

## Let community help you answer it

Purpose is rarely found alone in a room — it's discovered in the company of mentors, peers, and shared work. That's the quiet power of being part of a community project: you try things, get feedback, and notice what made you *feel* something.

The free courses on the KlagonOrg Learning Hub are also low-stakes experiments — a way to taste skills (AI, communication, business) before you commit. Each one teaches you something about what you care about.

Purpose isn't a prize at the end of a search. It's a direction you confirm by walking.

Start with one experiment, one honest look at your energy, and one small step. The clarity you're chasing is already hiding inside the things you keep doing well.
